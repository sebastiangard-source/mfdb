## Canali's "Rain & Wind" is not Loro Piana's

Asked narrowly, and the answer came back better than expected. **Not Loro Piana's**, on four pieces of evidence:

- The garments are **70% polyester / 30% viscose** and **100% polyester**. Loro Piana's textile division is a
  natural-fibre house; its Systems are finishes applied to Loro Piana cloth.
- The **Italian pages attribute no more than the English ones** — "con membrana Rain & Wind", no mill, no ®.
  The hypothesis that IT would attribute where EN does not is dead.
- **Canali coins its own fabric names in exactly this register**: Impeccabile, Nuvola, Leaftec ("exclusive to
  Canali ... resistant to wind, rain, and abrasion"). "Rain & Wind" and "Rain Protection" fit the pattern.
- **The contrast case settles it.** When Canali *does* use Loro Piana cloth, the garment is marked: Canali
  coats are listed and labelled "Canali Loro Piana Storm System". The Rain & Wind pieces carry no such mark.

Also worth knowing: **Rain&Wind System looks commercially dormant at Loro Piana.** Its live, promoted marks are
Storm System, Rain System, Green Storm System and Clima System; IR 1001500 reads as a defensive registration.

What would settle it beyond doubt and could not be reached: a photograph of a current garment's composition
label, and Loro Piana's B2B textile catalogue behind its trade wall.

**The consequence for your standing rule** is small but worth stating: the rule is sound, and the trigger for
it should be *marked attribution*, not name resemblance. Houses that license Loro Piana fabric mark it, because
the licence requires them to. A name that merely resembles a Loro Piana mark is the house's own descriptor
until a label says otherwise.

## The depth pilot: one brand, to size the job

You need a fraction — the Peter Millar number was 51 of 581 — and the screen cannot produce one. So I piloted
the measurement on **Southern Tide**, chosen because it is small enough to finish. It became a **full census,
not a sample: all 459 men's product pages read, no errors.**

| reading | count | share of 459 |
|---|---|---|
| Names a technology (Coastal Breeze, brrr°, Intercoastal) | 97 | 21.1% |
| Names a technology, broad (adds Driver, Cruiser, Tidal, Sandbar, Fairway) | 127 | 27.7% |
| Makes a performance claim, any | 311 | 67.8% |
| **Makes a performance claim and names nothing** | **215** | **46.8%** |
| Majority synthetic by composition | 109 | 23.7% |

The single-family comparison lands almost exactly on the precedent: **Coastal Breeze is 53 of 459 (11.5%)**
against Summer Comfort's 51 of 581 (8.8%). Same order of magnitude, which suggests the two numbers are
comparable and the method transfers.

**The finding that matters is the 215.** Nearly half the men's range claims performance while naming nothing at
all. A dial built on naming cannot see those products; a dial built on depth counts them. That is the
difference between the old rubric and the new one, measured on one brand.

**Cost: about 25 minutes, of which the data pull was 40 seconds.** Roughly 80% was discovery. Three traps for
whoever writes the depth brief:

1. **Colourways.** Southern Tide keeps colourways as variants (one polo had 55), so 459 counts styles. On a
   site with a page per colourway the same count inflates twenty- to fiftyfold.
2. **The platform's own count lies.** Shopify's `collections.json` reported 550 for a collection the live feed
   and the rendered page both put at 459. Use the number the storefront displays.
3. **Composition is usually not in the feed** — only 82 of 459 descriptions carried a percentage, which forced
   a full page crawl, and page HTML is full of non-fabric percentages ("110% store credit", "100% would
   recommend") that a bare regex will eat.

**What made this cheap will not transfer.** An open Shopify `products.json`, and Southern Tide's own
`Features:` and `Material:` tag discipline, did most of the work. Peter Millar is not Shopify. The transferable
core is only: find the denominator the site itself displays, establish whether colourways are products, then
count the numerator three ways.

One judgement call flagged for you: I excluded the Collegiate department (licensed gameday, largely men's cut).
Including it would take the denominator from 459 to about 1,120 and change every share.

No score is given or implied, here or in the pilot file.

Four things fall out of that table.

## What the depth brief will need to say

- **Fix the product unit first.** Shopify collapses colourways into styles; Herno's platform gives every
  colourway its own product ID; NN07's listing sits between the two (1,064 colourway URLs → 532 tiles → 242

Dries Van Noten (a 216-product census), Ferragamo (29 men's outerwear pages), McQueen (12), Scotch & Soda and

**3 of 8 is 37.5%, with a 95% interval of roughly 14–69%** — on 156 unflagged brands that is somewhere between

## The traps this pass turned up are worth more than the counts

Three of them would each have produced a confidently wrong row, and one of them invalidates work already done.

1. **A zero is worthless unless the search can produce one.** I started sending a nonsense string (`zzzqqxwv`)
   as a null control alongside the live control term. **Kiton returns 235 results for the nonsense string** —
   its search can never return zero, so every earlier "nothing found on Kiton" was meaningless as search
   evidence. Its row still stands, but on different evidence: product pages and its own fabrics page, which
   names only Kiton materials and Kiton's own mill. **Cesare Attolini's search returns "no results" for a live
   control term too** — the endpoint is dead and there is no catalogue behind the maintenance page.
2. **Exact-match searching breaks on Italianised spellings.** Rubinacci writes **"Storm Sistem"**. A term list
   containing "storm system" returns a clean zero on a site that plainly sells it. I re-ran the six Italian
   houses with the variant spellings: **Corneliani, Canali and Ring Jacket hold up** (each with a real
   zero against a live control, and Canali's index doesn't even contain the token "storm"), **Boglioli's zero
   is structural** — it names no mill anywhere in its copy, so its catalogue could not show a Loro Piana mark
   even if every jacket were Loro Piana — and **Kiton and Cesare Attolini are now unclear** for the reasons
   above.
3. **Two failures that fabricate their own evidence.** Ring Jacket **echoes the search query back as a page
   heading**, so searching for Loro Piana renders "Loro Piana Product List" — which reads exactly like a real
   brand category page. The null control exposed it ("zzzqqxwv Product List"). And a grep without word
   boundaries turns **"furthermore" into Thermore and "helperText" into Pertex** — that one produced phantom
   positives in a first pass before being caught, so any positive in this lineage that was found by substring
   matching rather than by opening a product page deserves a second look.
4. **Ferragamo is the accordion trap inverted.** Its product HTML contains no description at all — a
   server-side grep returns a clean zero, and so does a grep for the control words "nylon" and "wool". Only the
   rendered DOM has the copy. Checking a control word in the same channel you are searching is what catches it.

## What I would put in the next brief

- **Sample the unflagged population before spending another screen.** Thirty brands drawn at random from the
  156, swept the same way, would turn a 14–69% interval into something you could plan against. It is about
  five hours of the same work that produced this note.
- **Every negative row needs three controls**: a live control term (the search returns something), a null
  control (a nonsense string returns nothing), and a channel control (the word you are searching for could be
  found in the channel you are searching — server HTML, rendered DOM, or JSON feed). Any row missing these is
  an assumption, not a finding.
- **Search term lists need spelling variants** — Sistem/System, Italian and Japanese forms, and the house's own
  house-style renderings.
